Approximate computing emerges as a viable solution to enhance energy efficiency in applications sensitive
to human perception, particularly on edge devices. This work introduces a novel piece-wise approximate
floating-point divider that boasts resource efficiency and runtime configurability. Our method leverages a
piece-wise approximation algorithm for computing 1/y by exploiting powers of 2, complemented by an error
compensation technique grounded in thorough mathematical analysis. This approach facilitates the realiza-
tion of a reciprocal-based floating-point divider devoid of multipliers, which not only mitigates hardware re-
source consumption but also reduces latency. Additionally, we unveil a multi-level runtime configurable hard-
ware architecture that significantly improves flexibility across diverse application contexts. Compared to the
existing state-of-the-art approximate dividers and truncated exact dividers, our proposed solution achieves
a superior compromise between precision and resource efficiency. Application-level evaluations reveal that
our design provides over 87.7% energy saving while maintaining a negligible impact on output quality.

1   Introduction
Recently, with a notable surge of human perception-related tasks (e.g., audio/image processing,
machine learning), it has become evident that stringent precision and exactness are not always
necessary [5, 18, 31, 34]. Simultaneously, the rapid expansion of the Internet of Things (IoT) and
edge computing technologies has spotlighted the critical need for optimizing energy consumption,
given the inherent hardware limitations. This context has fueled interest in approximate com-
puting, an approach that prioritizes energy efficiency by compromising computational accuracy
[9, 22].
   In the quest for reduced energy consumption, the exploration of approximate arithmetic units,
including adders and multipliers, has shown potential in lowering power usage while retaining
acceptable levels of accuracy [15, 32, 33]. Division, however, stands out as an operation that typi-
cally requires more time, energy, and hardware resources than its counterparts [29]. For example,
an exact floating-point (FP) divider consumes over 15× more Area-Delay-Product (ADP)
and exhibits more than 1.5× higher power consumption compared to an exact FP multiplier [28].
Nevertheless, division plays a crucial role in a wide array of applications, from the OpenCV
multimedia library [4] to page-ranking normalization [12] and robotics control algorithms [8].
Therefore, the demand for resource-efficient dividers is significant, especially in contexts
where division is indispensable.
   Several studies have proposed various approximate divider designs. We can group them into
three main categories: subtractor-array-based [6, 7, 11, 14], logarithm-based [13, 19, 21, 27, 28],
and reciprocal-based dividers [3, 17, 20, 25, 29, 35]. While each type has its advantages, they also
come with limitations. Subtractor-array and reciprocal-based dividers can be very accurate, but
they often require substantial hardware overhead. Subtractor-array dividers encounter latency
issues due to their array structure [6, 7, 11, 14]. Reciprocal-based dividers always need one to three
multipliers to complete computations [3, 17, 20, 25, 35]. Despite these challenges, the above dividers
still manifest benefits in terms of hardware resource efficiency, especially when considering the
inherent complexity differences between multipliers and dividers. On the other hand, logarithm-
based dividers are more hardware-efficient but encounter difficulties in maintaining accuracy and
offering runtime configurability. They often rely on pre-loaded lookup tables (LUTs) for error
compensation, which significantly increases hardware resource requirements and makes runtime
adjustments impractical [13, 19].
   Obviously, designing an efficient and flexible approximate divider encompasses several chal-
lenges, as outlined below:

    — A prevalent issue with many current approximate division techniques is their lack of
      circuit-friendly design implementations. As a consequence, these approaches are often
      constrained by the inherent latency of subtractor arrays or the excessive hardware overheads
      associated with multiple multipliers.
    — Improving computational precision or incorporating error correction mechanisms
      proves challenging across existing approximate division methods. Attempts to reduce com-
      putational inaccuracies generally result in additional computational cycles or the need for
      substantial hardware enhancements, such as the incorporation of LUTs. These measures fre-
      quently negate the intended energy efficiency gains of approximate computing.
    — The need for runtime configurability is frequently disregarded. Many existing designs
      either do not support runtime adjustments or introduce prohibitive time and energy costs
      for such modifications. This limitation significantly constrains the versatility and practical
      utility of these approximate dividers.

   To tackle the aforementioned challenges, we propose PACE (Piece-wise Approximate and
ConfigurablE FP divider), a resource-efficient and runtime configurable design. Our major con-
tributions include:
    — We have developed a multiplier-free reciprocal-based floating-point divider that sub-
       stitutes the multiplication by 1/y with simpler operations such as shifts and additions,
       thereby reducing hardware resource consumption and decreasing latency.
    — We present a piece-wise approximation algorithm for the 1/y operation. This approach
       enhances the precision of division by increasing the number of piece-wise linear segments
       to approximately 1/y, thereby improving division accuracy with minimal complexity.
    — We propose an error compensation technique for our approximation algorithm, formu-
       lated through detailed mathematical analysis. This technique can be seamlessly incorporated
       into the algorithm without necessitating extra hardware resources.
    — Lastly, we have crafted a multi-level runtime configurable circuit to boost flexibility
       for various applications. This innovative structure allows for dynamic adjustments of
       the approximation level during operation, enabling tradeoff between delay and energy
       consumption.
   The experimental results demonstrate that PACE achieves a favorable tradeoff between accuracy
and resource utilization when compared to the other leading dividers in the field. Notably, PACE
matches the accuracy of the most precise approximate divider, QIAD [17], while only requiring
10.75% of the resources according to the ADP metric. Among alternatives, FaNZeD [28] is the only
divider that demands fewer hardware resources than PACE, yet it incurs errors approximately 5×
greater than those of PACE. At the application level, PACE exhibits only minimal degradation in
output quality, with average PSNR reductions of just 0.09 dB, 0.63 dB, and 0.27 dB for K-means
color quantization, JPEG compression, and Gaussian filtering, respectively. Moreover, it consumes
merely 12.3%, 9.5%, and 9.8% of the energy compared to an exact divider.
   This article is an extension to our prior work [30], including an enhanced error compensation
technique, an additional truncation mechanism, and more comprehensive experimental results.
The rest of the article is organized as follows: Section 2 provides a concise review of exact floating-
point division and the current state of research on approximate dividers. Sections 3 and 4 elaborate
on the proposed approximate division algorithm and its error compensation method, respectively.
The design of the multiplier-free divider is discussed in Section 5. Section 6 evaluates the accuracy,
resource efficiency, and performance of our design in comparison with existing work. Section 7
evaluates the impact of integrating PACE into real-world applications. Potential limitations of
PACE are discussed in Section 8, and concluding remarks are presented in Section 9.

   The non-restoring division method differs by always retaining the result of the subtraction, even
when it results in an incorrect partial remainder. Instead of restoring the previous state, this ap-
proach compensates by altering the next operation to addition, if the previous subtraction yields a
negative remainder. Quotient bits are determined from the set {1, −1}, where 1 indicates subtrac-
tion and −1 for addition, necessitating a subsequent conversion to normalize both the remainder
and the quotient. An advancement in this method is the SRT division [10], which reincorporates
0 (indicating no operation) into the quotient bit selection set. This inclusion permits the omission
of operations on certain iterations by directly shifting the partial remainder, thereby streamlining
the division process for improved efficiency.
   Algorithm 1 and its variants can be easily transformed into iterative divider circuits, which
function across multiple clock cycles. Alternatively, unfolding these iterations into a cell array
facilitates the creation of a purely combinational divider. Figure 1 illustrates the core structure of
a non-restoring array divider. This array consists of k × k cells, each capable of executing addition
or complementary subtraction. However, such dividers incur resource consumption proportional
to O(k 2 ) for k-bit quotients [26].
   The above discussions represent the digit recurrence division approach, favored for standalone
hardware implementations [23]. Other division algorithms fall into distinct categories, such as
functional iteration, high radix, table lookup, and their hybrids [24]. While these methods intro-
duce certain enhancements, they generally do not deviate from the fundamental complexity asso-
ciated with division. Throughout the remainder of this article, we consider the implementation of
an exact divider in the form of an array divider, given that the dividers examined later are primarily
combinational.

2.2   Approximate Divider
Given the substantial energy expenditure and computational complexity associated with exact
dividers, numerous researchers have shifted their focus to approximate dividers, exchanging effi-
ciency for accuracy. Here we summarize several common approximation strategies:
   Direct modification to the exact divider. This is a relatively intuitive strategy. Researches
cited in [11, 14] proposed a pruning mechanism on conventional dividers, diminishing the bit
width of the input following the detection of the leading one, thus simplifying the division pro-
cedure. Alternatively, studies such as [2, 6, 7] suggest the partial substitution of standard units
with approximate ones in restoring, non-restoring, and high-radix dividers. This modification per-
mits adjustable levels of approximation. However, these modifications are still constrained by the
underlying principles of precise division, inheriting its hardware redundancy while possessing
less-than-ideal approximation performance.
   Utilize the logarithmic conversion. A typical logarithm-based approximate divider under-
goes three phases: logarithmic transformation, subtraction, and inverse logarithmic conversion.
In this way, the division is transformed into a simpler subtraction in the logarithmic domain. To
facilitate binary computations, logarithmic operations are usually approximated as linear func-
tions, known as the Mitchell Method [21], outlined in Equation (3):

                                              log N = k + log(1 + x) ≈ k + x,                                         (3)

where N = 2k (1 + x), 0  x < 1. Despite its simplicity, the original Mitchell Method can intro-
duce non-negligible errors. Several studies introduced enhancements based on it, such as FaNZeD
[28] and LEAD [27], which employ compensation coefficients and exponential series expansions,
respectively, to refine accuracy.
   Convert to multiplication with the reciprocal. Given that multiplication typically requires
less resources [17], another approach to calculating the division involves converting the opera-
tion A/B into A × (1/B). Several techniques for calculating the reciprocal 1/B have been explored.
For instance, ILAFD [25] applies direct bit-wise inversion, while TruncApp [29] adopts a specific
approximation method shown in Equation (4):
                                                        

   While straightforward, these methods often yield higher errors and generally rely on LUTs for
parameter storage or error compensation. Therefore, recent reciprocal-based studies have delved
into mathematical strategies for approximating 1/x. For example, Taylor expansions, Newton–
Raphson iterations, and quadratic curve fitting are employed in SAADI-EC [20], ADNR-EC [3],
and QIAD [17], respectively. These approaches achieve notable precision but usually require ei-
ther several multipliers within a single cycle or additional cycles if limited to one multiplier. Such
requirements increase the complexity of the divider design.
   In conclusion, although significant progress has been made in the field, finding an approximate
divider that optimally balances precision with resource efficiency continues to be a formidable
challenge.

5.2     Design for Runtime Configurability
We introduce a new tree structure to efficiently calculate PS. This structure uses 3-to-2 compressor
units (represented by brackets in Figure 7) to implement equation Equation (14). This design allows
the circuit to dynamically switch between different approximate levels. Each dot in the figure
represents a partial sum, while squares and triangles represent the sum and carry outputs of the
compressors, respectively. Dotted arrows indicate direct connections that pass PS forward.
   Notably, the tree features four output nodes, encased in red rectangles, aligning with approxi-
mation levels 1, 2, and levels 3 and 4, which include the error compensation term c 3 . This design
allows the system to efficiently operate at lower approximate levels without necessarily traversing
the entire tree. This optimization reduces delay in the critical path and enhances overall efficiency.
The inherent flexibility of this approach makes it well-suited for applications demanding runtime
configurability.

6 Experimental Results
In a comprehensive evaluation, we compared the proposed PACE divider across four accuracy
levels (L1–L4)∗ with truncated exact dividers and several state-of-the-art approximate dividers
from prior works. The evaluation includes tests on accuracy, resource usage, and performance.
   When measuring accuracy, 10,000 data points were uniformly sampled within the normalized
range [1, 2) for both divisors x and dividends y, given that PACE and other dividers haven’t im-
plemented any unconventional approaches in the exponent part. Four error metrics were used for
evaluation: Mean Error (ME), Mean Absolute Error (MAE), Mean Relative Error Distance
(MRED), and RMSE.
   For hardware-related metrics, all designs were implemented in Verilog HDL. Synthesises were
performed using Synopsys Design Compiler with UMC’s 40-nm library, using consistent design
constraints and options across all implementations.

6.1    Comparison with Truncated Exact Divider
To assess the effectiveness of PACE’s truncation mechanism, we compared it to a baseline imple-
mentation where we simply truncated the mantissa of the exact 32-bit floating-point divider from
the Synopsys DesignWare library.
   We sequentially applied truncation, removing 0, 4, 8, 12, 14, 15, 16, 17, and 18 last significant
bits from the mantissa part. This sequence prioritizes reducing computational complexity during
the initial, flatter portion of the error curve, while still maintaining accuracy as the curve becomes
steeper. The maximum number of truncation bits for each PACE level is ultimately limited by the
minimum bit requirement of the error compensation mechanism.
   The tradeoff between accuracy (RMSE) and resource utilization (ADP) for PACE and the exact
divider with varying truncation widths is visualized in Figure 8. First, we investigated the relation-
ship between the number of truncated bits and PACE calculation accuracy. Our analysis revealed
a consistent error trend across all approximation levels: truncating fewer than 12 bits (retaining
at least 12 bits in the mantissa) resulted in minimal RMSE increase, while further truncation led

7.1 Accuracy
In K-means Color Quantization, we incorporated the approximate dividers into the standard clus-
ter averaging operation performed in each K-means iteration, as done by Ratnaparkhi et al. [27].
This operation relies heavily on division. For JPEG image compression, similar to Saadat et al. [28],
we replaced the element-wise division within the Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) step with
the approximate dividers. This substitution affects the quantization of DCT coefficients, a crucial
step in JPEG compression. And in Gaussian filter, divisions implemented with approximate di-
viders are mainly used to compute the two-dimensional Gaussian function applied to each pixel
for image smoothing. For these algorithms that rely on approximate division as a crucial interme-
diate result, the quality of the application output is directly linked to the accuracy and bias of the
approximate division. Metrics such as MAE, MRED, and RMSE measure relative error, while ME
evaluates unbiasedness. Generally, smaller error values and lower bias lead to better application
output quality. These applications are evaluated on the Caltech101 dataset [16], which contains
102 categories and 9,144 images in total.
   Figure 11 provides a glance at the visual quality of a sample image processed by the selected
three multimedia applications, using either the exact divider or our proposed PACE divider.
   To quantify the impact on image quality for comparison, we measured the difference in Peak
Signal-to-Noise Ratio (PSNR), between images processed with approximate and exact dividers.
This difference is denoted as PSNR degradation, calculated as: PSNRdegradation = PSNRaccurate −
PSNRapprox .
   The statistics of PSNR degradation due to the use of approximate dividers on the Caltech101
dataset are demonstrated in Figure 12. It is obvious that a higher level of PACE leads to better im-
age quality. In comparison with others, PACE(L2) outperforms most other approximate dividers
for all three applications, except QIAD. At level 4, PACE exhibits minimal PSNR reduction, averag-
ing only 0.09 dB, 0.63 dB, and 0.27 dB for K-means color quantization, JPEG, and Gaussian Filter,
respectively. When compared to the most accurate QIAD divider among prior works, PACE(L4)
achieves superior quality in all three applications, while requiring significantly fewer resources
and incurring lower latency.

8 Discussion and Limitations
Although PACE has significant advantages over state-of-the-art approximate dividers in terms of
approximate accuracy, configurability, and low hardware overhead, it still has some shortcomings:
   — Accuracy Improvement. While PACE employs knt , a power-of-2-based approximation of
     1/y, to simplify hardware implementation, it’s important to note that knt actually approxi-
     mates (0.5y 2 − 2y + 2.5). This limitation prevents continuous accuracy improvement through
     higher approximation levels, ultimately restricting PACE’s accuracy to a level below that of
     a precise divider.
   — Unbiasedness Design. To address potential bias in the approximate algorithm, we con-
     sidered the impact of knt ’s approximation of 1/y. Since knt is inherently biased (knt < ynt ),
     multiplying by (x − y) helps to symmetrize the deviation around 1. However, due to the ac-
     tual range of x/y being (1/2, 2), complete unbiasedness remains unattainable, as evidenced
     by the experimental data in Section 6.2.
   — Inefficiency for Low Levels. While PACE(L4) offers exceptional accuracy with minimal
     hardware overhead, PACE(L1) may not be the most efficient choice for applications with
     a high tolerance for division errors. Compared to FaNZeD [28], PACE(L1) exhibits slightly
     higher overhead in terms of area, latency, and energy consumption. This suggests that other
     approximate dividers may be more suitable for scenarios where accuracy is less critical.

9 Conclusion
This work introduces PACE, a novel piece-wise approximate floating-point divider. Combining
approximation and error correction, the proposed approach creates a multiplier-free, multi-level
circuit with runtime configurability and adjustable accuracy. Compared to existing dividers, PACE
offers a superior balance between accuracy and resource usage. Application-level evaluations show
over 87.7% energy savings with PACE compared to an exact divider, with a marginal decline in
output quality.
